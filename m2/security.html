Experiment 06

Security analysis

These notes connect the earlier experiments to the attacks they resist. This lab is still not a production login system: real services also need HTTPS, rate limiting and server-side hashing.

High risk

Plaintext storage

Storing passwords exactly as entered means anyone who obtains the database can read every credential, and users who reused the password elsewhere are exposed too.

Defence. Never store plaintext. Use a dedicated password-hashing algorithm with a unique salt.
Common

Dictionary attacks

An attacker tries likely passwords from wordlists, common phrases and leaked credentials. Human-chosen passwords follow predictable patterns.

Defence. Prefer long unique passphrases and reject known-compromised passwords.
Compute

Brute-force attacks

Every candidate is tried until one matches. The search space grows with length and character variety, but fast hashes keep each guess cheap.

Defence. Use slow, tunable password hashing such as bcrypt, Argon2id or scrypt.
Precomputed

Rainbow tables

Precomputed hash chains speed up recovery of unsalted passwords when the algorithm and candidate space are known.

Defence. A unique random salt per password makes shared precomputed tables ineffective.
Essential

Salting

A salt is a random unique value combined with the password before hashing, so the same password yields different stored outputs for different accounts.

Key point. Salts are not secret. Store each salt with its derived hash. bcrypt embeds the salt in its output string.
Recommended

Why bcrypt?

bcrypt includes a salt and a configurable cost factor. Deliberately slow computation raises the price of every guess, unlike fast SHA functions.

Key point. For new systems consider Argon2id. bcrypt remains a widely deployed option with a high enough cost.

Brute-force estimator

Average time to find a random password. Rates are order-of-magnitude assumptions for one modern GPU, not measurements.
